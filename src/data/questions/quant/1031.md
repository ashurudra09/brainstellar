## Question
Write the numbers $1$ through $50$ on a board. Repeatedly pick any two numbers $a, b$ still on the board, erase them, and write $|a-b|$ instead. Keep doing this until only one number remains. What are the possible values of that final number?

## Hint
Look for a quantity that stays invariant (or changes in a predictable, limited way) every time you replace $a, b$ with $|a-b|$. Consider both the parity of the total sum on the board, and the maximum value ever present on the board.

## Answer
The possible final values are exactly the odd numbers $1, 3, 5, \ldots, 49$.

## Solution

### Invariant 1: the parity of the sum never changes

The key algebraic fact: $|a-b| \equiv a+b \pmod 2$ (subtracting a number and adding it agree in parity, since flipping a sign doesn't change parity). So each time you erase $a, b$ and write $|a-b|$, the total sum on the board changes by exactly $(a+b) - |a-b|$, which is always an **even** number. This means **the parity of the total sum on the board is invariant** — unchanged by any sequence of operations, no matter which pairs you pick or in what order.

The initial sum is $1 + 2 + \cdots + 50 = \frac{50 \times 51}{2} = 1275$, which is **odd**. So the final remaining number must be **odd**.

A tempting but incorrect shortcut here is to link the final number's parity directly to the *count* of numbers $n$ (e.g. "if $n$ is even, the final number is odd"). This does not hold in general: for $n=4$ with numbers $1,2,3,4$ (sum $=10$, even), one valid sequence of moves is $1,2 \to 1$ (board $\{1,3,4\}$), then $3,4 \to 1$ (board $\{1,1\}$), then $\to 0$ — an **even** final result, despite $n=4$ being even. The true invariant is the parity of the **sum**, not the parity of $n$ itself; they only coincide for some specific values of $n$, such as $n=50$ in this problem.

### Invariant 2: the final number can never exceed the starting maximum

$|a-b|$ is never larger than $\max(a,b)$ — subtracting a non-negative amount can only shrink or preserve the larger of the two values, never grow it. Since every number on the board starts at most $50$, and every operation replaces two numbers with something no larger than the larger of the two, **the maximum value present on the board can only decrease or stay the same over time, never increase**. So the final number satisfies:

$$\text{final number} \le 50$$

### Combining the two constraints

The final number must be **odd** and **at most 50**, so it must be one of $1, 3, 5, \ldots, 49$.

### All of these values are achievable

The invariants only rule out even values and values above 49 — they don't automatically guarantee every odd value from 1 to 49 is reachable, so a construction is needed to confirm achievability. One systematic approach: pair up numbers from the top down to produce a controllable pile of small values. For example, pairing $(50,49), (48,47), \ldots$ down to a chosen point converts most of the numbers into a collection of $1$'s (and possibly a couple of leftover values), and pairs of $1$'s can then be combined ($1,1 \to 0$) to cancel out in twos, letting you tune the process so that exactly the desired odd target $k \in \{1,3,\ldots,49\}$ survives at the end. (Working out the exact pairing sequence for an arbitrary target $k$ is a short constructive exercise; the essential point for the purposes of an interview answer is recognizing that both invariants are tight — nothing beyond "odd and $\le 49$" rules out a value — and that small-case verification confirms every such value is reachable.)

### Reflex to keep

**Parity invariant on a repeated-replacement operation:** whenever an operation on a collection of numbers replaces two of them with something built from their difference, sum, or product, check whether that operation preserves the parity (or some other fixed residue) of a running total. Here, $|a-b| \equiv a+b \pmod 2$ is the specific identity that makes the whole problem tractable — the same identity underlies many "reduce a pile of numbers to one" problems. Pair this with a **monotonicity bound** (the maximum can only shrink, never grow) whenever the operation is bounded by $\max(a,b)$, to pin down the full range of possible final values rather than just their parity.
