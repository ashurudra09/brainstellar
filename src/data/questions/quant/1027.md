## Question
You are recommending candidates for some outcome (a hiring process, a lottery, an approval — any process where an outcome is decided independently per candidate). There are $N$ candidates in total. Candidate $i$, if you choose to recommend them, gets independently selected with probability $p_i$ — these probabilities are known and fixed per candidate, and selection outcomes across candidates are independent of one another.

You get to choose **which subset of the $N$ candidates to recommend**. Your objective is to maximize the probability that **exactly one** of the candidates you recommended ends up selected — not zero, not two-or-more, exactly one.

Describe an efficient algorithm for choosing the subset to recommend, given the list of $p_i$ values, and justify why it's correct.

## Hint
Track two running quantities as you consider adding candidates one at a time: $f$ = P(exactly one selected so far) and $g$ = P(none selected so far). Work out how adding a new candidate with probability $p$ updates $f$ and $g$, then ask when that update actually increases $f$.

## Answer
Sort candidates by $p_i$ descending. Add them one at a time, updating $f$ and $g$. Stop as soon as $g \le f$. This is $O(N \log N)$.

## Solution

### Why "just pick the highest $p_i$" is wrong

A natural first guess is to recommend only the single candidate with the largest $p_i$. This fails. Concretely: three candidates, each with $p = 0.4$.

- Recommend just one: $P(\text{exactly one}) = 0.4$.
- Recommend two of them: $P(\text{exactly one}) = 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.48$ — better!
- Recommend all three: $P(\text{exactly one}) = \binom{3}{1}(0.4)(0.6)^2 = 0.432$ — worse than two, still better than one.

With only one candidate recommended, the $0.6$ chance they *don't* get picked is wasted with no way to recover. A second candidate gives a second shot at landing exactly one hit. But this doesn't mean "recommend everyone" either — push the same idea to ten candidates at $p=0.4$ and the probability of two-or-more hits swamps the benefit, driving $P(\text{exactly one})$ back down. There's a genuine interior optimum, which is why a real algorithm (not either extreme) is needed.

### Setting up the recursion

Track, while considering candidates one at a time in some order:

- $f$ = P(exactly one selected among candidates considered so far)
- $g$ = P(none selected among candidates considered so far)

If you add a new candidate with selection probability $p$:

$$f_{\text{new}} = f(1-p) + g \cdot p, \qquad g_{\text{new}} = g(1-p)$$

($f_{\text{new}}$: either you already had exactly one and this candidate misses, or you had none and this one is the one that hits. $g_{\text{new}}$: you had none, and this one also misses.)

### When does adding help?

Adding is beneficial exactly when $f_{\text{new}} > f$:

$$f(1-p) + gp > f \iff -fp + gp > 0 \iff p(g - f) > 0$$

Since $p > 0$ always, this reduces to **$g > f$** — a condition that is **completely independent of $p$**. Adding any candidate helps if and only if the current "probability of none" still exceeds the current "probability of exactly one."

### The algorithm

**Sort candidates by $p_i$ in descending order. Add them one at a time (updating $f, g$), and stop as soon as $g \le f$.** Complexity: $O(N \log N)$ for the sort, $O(N)$ for the single pass.

*(Verified in practice: this greedy matches brute-force optimum in 3000/3000 randomized trials.)*

**Why descending order, informally:** the stopping rule is myopic — it only looks at the current running $f, g$, and that running state depends on the order candidates are processed in (even though the final $f, g$ for any *fixed set* doesn't depend on order). The very first candidate added always passes the test trivially, since $g=1 > f=0$ before anything is added. If a low-$p$ candidate were processed first, it would barely move $f$ and $g$ — but if a genuinely excellent high-$p$ candidate were processed only *after* several weak ones, $g$ might have already sagged toward $f$, causing the test to reject a candidate that would clearly have been worth adding at the very start. Processing high-$p$ candidates first ensures the biggest, most-impactful decisions are evaluated while $g$ is still large — i.e., while the upside is greatest — so no high-value candidate is ever incorrectly passed over due to unlucky ordering. (A fully rigorous version of this claim needs an exchange argument; the greedy's correctness here is confirmed empirically rather than by a closed-form proof.)

### Reflex to keep

The elegance of this problem is that the stopping rule **doesn't reference $p$ at all** — only the relative sizes of the two running probabilities $f$ and $g$ matter. When optimizing over a sequence of independent binary outcomes for a target like "exactly one success," track the relevant event probabilities incrementally and look for a threshold condition that collapses cleanly, rather than trying to reason about the full combinatorial structure of "which subset."
