## Question
You have 200 whole numbers. Prove that some non-empty subset of them sums to a multiple of 200.

## Hint
Build the sequence of prefix sums and look at each one modulo 200. How many possible remainders are there, and how many prefix sums do you have?

## Answer
Such a subset always exists, and in fact a contiguous one can always be found.

## Solution

### Construction

Label the 200 numbers $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_{200}$ in any fixed order. Build the **prefix sums**:

$$S_0 = 0, \quad S_1 = a_1, \quad S_2 = a_1 + a_2, \quad \ldots, \quad S_{200} = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_{200}$$

That's **201 values** ($S_0$ through $S_{200}$).

### Pigeonhole

Look at each $S_i$ **modulo 200** — there are only **200 possible residues** ($0, 1, \ldots, 199$).

With 201 values and only 200 residue classes, **two of the prefix sums must share the same residue mod 200**. Say $S_j \equiv S_k \pmod{200}$ with $j < k$.

### Conclusion

Then:

$$S_k - S_j = a_{j+1} + a_{j+2} + \cdots + a_k$$

is divisible by 200 — and it's a genuine **non-empty, contiguous** block of the original numbers (since $j < k$).

So a subset summing to a multiple of 200 always exists — in fact, a **contiguous** one, which is a stronger conclusion than the problem even asked for.

### A note on the numbers' size

This proof never used any upper bound on the individual numbers $a_i$ (such as "each is at most 200") — it holds for *any* 200 whole numbers, regardless of how large they are. This is a common feature of pigeonhole proofs: a bound given in a problem statement often turns out to be unnecessary, or weaker than what's actually needed for the argument to go through.

### Reflex to keep

**"Does some subset sum to a multiple of $n$?" → build prefix sums, then pigeonhole on residues mod $n$.** This is one of the most reusable tricks in combinatorics: with $n$ numbers, you get $n+1$ prefix sums (including the empty one, $S_0 = 0$) but only $n$ possible residues mod $n$, guaranteeing a collision — and any collision between two prefix sums immediately hands you a contiguous subsequence summing to a multiple of $n$.
