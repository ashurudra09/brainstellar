## Question
A building has several chambers, each holding at least $3$ light bulbs. The total number of bulbs across all chambers is even. There is also a set of switches; each switch is wired to exactly $2$ bulbs (possibly in different chambers), and flipping it toggles both of those bulbs. All bulbs start off.

Prove that you can choose a subset of switches to flip so that, afterward, every single chamber has **some bulbs on and some bulbs off** (never all-on, never all-off, in any chamber).

## Hint
"Each switch touches exactly 2 bulbs" should make you think of graph edges. Model bulbs as vertices and switches as edges — then the goal becomes: find a subset of edges (a 2-colouring: chosen vs. not chosen) so that every chamber has vertices of both colours among its bulbs. Eulerian circuits give you a clean way to 2-colour edges.

## Answer
Such a subset of switches always exists — constructed via a dummy vertex plus an Eulerian-circuit edge 2-colouring.

## Solution

### Setting up the graph

Since every switch toggles exactly two bulbs, model each **bulb as a vertex** and each **switch as an edge** connecting the two bulbs it controls (a multigraph — parallel edges and edges within the same chamber are both allowed). Flipping a chosen set of switches corresponds to choosing a subset of edges; a bulb ends up **on** exactly when it is touched by an **odd** number of chosen edges.

Equivalently: 2-colour every switch (edge) red or blue, where "red" means "flip this switch." A bulb's final state is on iff it's touched by an odd number of red edges. The goal — every chamber has both an on bulb and an off bulb — becomes: **find a red/blue edge-colouring such that every chamber contains vertices with different final states.**

### The construction

1. **Fix parity.** Some chambers' bulbs may have odd total degree in this switch-graph. By the handshake lemma, the number of odd-degree vertices is even, so add a **dummy vertex** and connect it to every odd-degree vertex with one new edge. Now every vertex (in the graph including the dummy) has even degree.

2. **Eulerian circuit.** A connected graph where every vertex has even degree has an Eulerian circuit (a closed walk using every edge exactly once). Take such a circuit in each connected component and **colour its edges alternately red and blue** as you traverse it.

3. **Why this balances every chamber.** Because the colouring alternates strictly along the circuit, every time the circuit passes *through* a vertex, it uses one incoming edge and one outgoing edge of different colours (one red, one blue). So every vertex accumulates roughly equal numbers of red and blue edges among its incident edges in the circuit — at least, none of its edges can be monochromatic if degree $\ge 4$, since the visits alternate colour on each pass.

4. **Remove the dummy.** Delete the dummy vertex and its incident (dummy) edges. Each real bulb loses **at most one** edge (it was connected to the dummy at most once). This is where the **"$\ge 3$ bulbs per chamber"** condition earns its keep: after step 1, every vertex has degree $\ge 4$ (in particular, chambers with $\ge 3$ bulbs force enough total degree that, post dummy-pairing, degree $\ge 4$), which guarantees **at least 2 edges of each colour** before deletion. Removing one edge can't wipe out a colour entirely — each vertex retains at least one red and one blue edge.

5. **Conclude.** Turn on exactly the bulbs touched by an odd number of red edges (equivalently: flip the switches corresponding to red edges). Since every chamber's bulbs collectively see both colours, and colour determines final on/off status via parity of red touches, every chamber ends up with a genuine mix — never all on, never all off.

### Why the conditions matter

- **"Total bulbs is even"** is what makes the handshake-lemma pairing of odd-degree vertices with a single dummy vertex consistent (the dummy itself needs even degree too, which the even-count condition helps guarantee).
- **"$\ge 3$ bulbs per chamber"** is exactly the safety margin that survives the one-edge loss from deleting the dummy — with only 2 bulbs (degree 2 pre-fix), a chamber could end up monochromatic after the dummy edge is removed.

### Reflex to keep

Whenever a problem states "each switch/domino/rule touches exactly 2 things," model those 2 things as an edge in a graph. When the goal is "every group has a mix of two outcomes," reach for an **edge 2-colouring**, and Eulerian circuits are a standard tool for producing one for free by alternating colour along the tour.
