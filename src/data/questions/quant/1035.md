## Question
There are 7 identical cars, each with a full tank holding exactly 700 km worth of fuel. All of them start together at one end of a long, straight highway and begin driving in the same direction. At any point on the journey, fuel may be transferred instantaneously from one car to another, subject only to tank capacity — no car can ever hold more than 700 km of fuel. A car that runs dry is simply abandoned where it stands.

The goal is to get **one** car as far down the highway as possible. Devise a strategy, and find the maximum distance that single car can reach.

## Hint
The total fuel in the system is $7 \times 700 = 4900$ km-units, but tank capacity stops you from pouring it all into one car. Note that while $n$ cars drive together they all burn fuel at the same rate, so they all have the same amount of empty space in their tanks. Ask: after driving a distance $d$ with $n$ full cars, how large must $d$ be for one car's remaining fuel to *exactly* fill the empty space in the other $n-1$ tanks? Dumping too early wastes capacity; dumping too late means the abandoned car still has fuel in it.

## Answer
$$D = 700\left(1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac16+\frac17\right) = 700\,H_7 = 700\cdot\frac{363}{140} = 1815 \text{ km}$$

## Solution

### 1. The right moment to abandon a car
Keep the whole surviving fleet driving together, and drop a car exactly when its remaining fuel can be poured off with nothing spilled and nothing left behind.

Suppose $n$ cars set off with full tanks. After driving a distance $d$, every car has burned $d$, so every car holds $700-d$ and has empty space $d$. Abandoning one car means emptying its $700-d$ into the other $n-1$ cars, whose combined empty space is $(n-1)d$. For a perfect fit:

$$700 - d = (n-1)d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = \frac{700}{n}$$

At that instant the remaining $n-1$ cars are *again* brimming full, so the same argument applies recursively with $n-1$ cars.

### 2. The schedule
Each leg is $700/n$ long, with $n$ running from 7 down to 1:

| Cars running | Leg length (km) | Odometer at drop (km) | State after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | $700/7 = 100$ | 100 | 6 cars full |
| 6 | $700/6 \approx 116.67$ | $\approx 216.67$ | 5 cars full |
| 5 | $700/5 = 140$ | $\approx 356.67$ | 4 cars full |
| 4 | $700/4 = 175$ | $\approx 531.67$ | 3 cars full |
| 3 | $700/3 \approx 233.33$ | 765 | 2 cars full |
| 2 | $700/2 = 350$ | 1115 | 1 car full |
| 1 | $700$ | — | arrives at **1815** |

Total distance:

$$D = \frac{700}{7}+\frac{700}{6}+\dots+\frac{700}{1} = 700\sum_{j=1}^{7}\frac1j = 700\cdot\frac{363}{140} = 1815 \text{ km}$$

### 3. Fuel accounting check
On the leg with $n$ cars, $n$ cars each burn $700/n$ km of fuel, so every leg consumes exactly 700 units. Seven legs burn $7\times700 = 4900$ — the entire fuel supply, with nothing stranded in an abandoned car and nothing spilled. That is a necessary condition for optimality, though not by itself sufficient, since burning all the fuel efficiently also requires burning it with as few cars running as possible.

### 4. Why you cannot do better
Let $L_k$ be the total distance driven while exactly $k$ cars are still running, so $D=\sum_{k=1}^{7}L_k$. At the moment only $k$ cars remain, those $k$ cars hold at most $700k$ fuel (capacity), and every kilometre from then on is covered by $k$, then $k-1$, ..., then 1 car. Hence for every $k$:

$$\sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,L_j \;\le\; 700k$$

Maximising $\sum_j L_j$ subject to these seven constraints is a linear program. Weight constraint $k$ by

$$y_k = \frac1k - \frac1{k+1} \;\;(k\le 6), \qquad y_7 = \frac17,$$

so that the coefficient of each $L_j$ becomes $\sum_{k\ge j} j\,y_k = j\cdot\frac1j = 1$. The resulting bound is

$$D \;\le\; 700\sum_{k=1}^{7} k\,y_k \;=\; 700\left[\sum_{k=1}^{6}\left(1-\frac{k}{k+1}\right)+1\right] \;=\; 700\sum_{j=1}^{7}\frac1j \;=\; 1815$$

The schedule in step 2 attains this bound, so 1815 km is optimal.

### 5. Generalisation
With $n$ cars of range $R$ the answer is $R\,H_n$, where $H_n$ is the $n$-th harmonic number. Since $H_n \sim \ln n$, extra cars buy only logarithmically more distance: an 8th car here would add just $700/8 = 87.5$ km. This is a discrete cousin of the classic *jeep problem* (or "exploration problem"), whose one-way variant has the same harmonic answer.
