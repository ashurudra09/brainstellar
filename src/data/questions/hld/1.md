## Question
Design a system like bit.ly: given a long URL, return a short code that redirects to it. Support ~100M new URLs/day and a read:write ratio around 100:1.

## Requirements
- Short codes must be unique and not guessable in sequence (avoid enumeration).
- Redirect latency should be very low (it's on the hot path for every click).
- Custom aliases are a nice-to-have, not required for v1.
- 100M writes/day and ~10B reads/day at that ratio.

## Hint
The interesting part isn't "how do I generate a short string" -- plenty of ways do that. It's how you make ~10B reads/day cheap without hammering your primary datastore.

## Approach
- Encode a 64-bit auto-increment id (from a sharded counter/Snowflake-style generator, not a single DB sequence) into base62 for the short code -- gives uniqueness without a global lock.
- Store `code -> long_url` in a key-value store (e.g. DynamoDB/Cassandra) partitioned by code; that access pattern is a perfect fit for a KV store.
- Put a cache (Redis) in front of the datastore for reads, since the ratio is 100:1 -- most redirects should never touch the DB.
- Redirect service is stateless and horizontally scaled behind a load balancer; cache-miss falls through to the KV store and populates the cache.

## Trade-offs
Base62-encoding a counter is simpler and shorter than hashing the URL, but leaks the insertion order (sequential codes), which is the "not guessable" requirement above -- mitigated by not exposing the counter directly (e.g. XOR/permute it, or shard counters across many ranges) rather than switching to a hash, which would need a collision-retry loop instead.

## Solution
The system is read-heavy by two orders of magnitude, so the design centers on making reads cheap: a cache absorbs the vast majority of the 10B/day redirects, and the KV store only needs to handle cache misses plus the comparatively tiny write volume. The id-generation choice (sharded counter vs. UUID vs. hash-of-URL) matters less for scale than for the two real constraints it has to satisfy: no collisions without a retry loop, and no easily-enumerable sequence exposed to the public.
