## Question
Explain the bias-variance tradeoff, and describe what happens to each as you increase model complexity.

## Hint
Bias is about being systematically wrong; variance is about being inconsistently wrong across different training sets.

## Answer
Bias (error from overly simplistic assumptions) decreases as model complexity increases; variance (error from sensitivity to the specific training data) increases. Total expected error is the sum of both plus irreducible noise, so there's a complexity sweet spot that minimizes it.

## Solution
A high-bias model (e.g. linear regression on a nonlinear relationship) makes strong assumptions and underfits -- it's consistently wrong in the same way regardless of which training sample you give it. A high-variance model (e.g. a very deep decision tree) fits its training data closely, including its noise, so it changes a lot from one training sample to the next and generalizes poorly -- it overfits.

Expected test error decomposes as $\text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible Noise}$. As complexity increases, bias falls (the model can represent more) but variance rises (it has more freedom to fit noise), so the total typically forms a U-shape with a minimum somewhere in between -- which is exactly what techniques like regularization, cross-validation, and early stopping are trying to locate: the complexity level where you're not leaving easy signal on the table (too much bias) but also not chasing noise (too much variance).
