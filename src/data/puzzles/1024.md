## Question
A random process moves on the states $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$. From any state $i \ge 1$, it moves up to $i+1$ with probability $0.2$, down to $i-1$ with probability $0.3$, and stays at $i$ with probability $0.5$. From state $0$ specifically, it moves up to $1$ with probability $0.2$ and stays at $0$ with probability $0.8$.

What fraction of time does this process spend at state $0$ in the long run?

## Hint
This is a birth-death chain (every move is $\pm 1$ or "stay"). For chains like this, you don't need the full transition matrix — use detailed balance: in the long run, the rate of crossing from $i$ to $i+1$ must equal the rate of crossing back from $i+1$ to $i$. The "stay" probabilities never appear in this equation.

## Answer
1/3

## Solution

### Why a steady state exists at all

Before computing anything, it's worth checking that a stable long-run distribution exists in the first place, since the state space here is infinite. A Markov chain converges to a unique long-run distribution when it is:

1. **Irreducible** — every state can reach every other state. Here, the chain can move up or down freely, so yes.
2. **Aperiodic** — it isn't locked into a rigid cycle. The nonzero "stay" probabilities guarantee this immediately.
3. **Positive recurrent** — the chain doesn't drift off to infinity forever; it returns to low states often enough.

Point 3 is the one that actually needs checking on an infinite state space, and it hinges on the numbers given: away from state $0$, the process moves down with probability $0.3$ and up with probability $0.2$. Since $0.3 > 0.2$, there's a net *downward drift* whenever the chain is away from the boundary — like a random walk biased back toward $0$. This is what prevents the chain from wandering off to infinity and guarantees a genuine, non-degenerate stationary distribution exists. (If instead the up-probability were $\ge$ the down-probability, the chain would drift outward forever and the long-run fraction of time at any fixed state would be $0$ — no valid stationary distribution.)

### Detailed balance

Because this is a birth-death chain (moves are strictly $\pm 1$ or a self-loop), draw an imaginary boundary between state $i$ and state $i+1$. In the long run, every time the process crosses that boundary going up, it must eventually cross back going down — otherwise probability mass would keep accumulating on one side forever, contradicting the fact that the long-run fractions $\pi_i$ have settled to fixed values. So the *rate* of up-crossings must equal the *rate* of down-crossings:

$$\pi_i \cdot P(\text{up from } i) = \pi_{i+1} \cdot P(\text{down from } i+1)$$

Self-loops (the "stay" probabilities $0.5$ and $0.8$) never cross the boundary, so they simply don't appear in this equation — they're red herrings for this particular question.

### Solving

With $P(\text{up}) = 0.2$ and $P(\text{down}) = 0.3$ for every relevant state:

$$\pi_i \cdot 0.2 = \pi_{i+1} \cdot 0.3 \implies \pi_{i+1} = \frac{2}{3}\pi_i$$

So $\pi_i = \pi_0 \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^i$, a geometric sequence. Normalizing:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \pi_i = \pi_0 \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^i = \pi_0 \cdot \frac{1}{1 - 2/3} = 3\pi_0 = 1$$

$$\implies \pi_0 = \frac{1}{3}$$

*(Simulated confirmation: long-run fraction at state 0 comes out to approximately 0.330.)*

### Takeaway

For a birth-death chain, skip building the full transition matrix — detailed balance across each boundary gives you the ratio between consecutive $\pi_i$ directly, and self-loop ("stay") probabilities are irrelevant to that ratio. Always sanity-check first that the up/down probabilities create a net inward drift; without it, no stationary distribution exists to solve for.
